## The bed problem

You'd think the first problem when moving in together is whose sofa wins. It isn't. The first problem is the bedroom.

The bedroom is not small. It's *cozy*. Specifically, it's cozy in the space between the bed and the closets, which is where your shins live at 3 AM when you get up to pee.

So the question was: can we dare a 180 cm bed, or do we have to settle for a 160?

## Blender first

The plan was simple. Claude would build the apartment in Blender, the 3D software, and give me an isometric view, like the base in XCOM. Then I could swap beds and closets around and see what fits.

I have never opened Blender in my life. I still haven't. Claude drove it entirely through Python scripts, which is the kind of sentence that would have made 2019 me spit out my coffee.

## The pipeline

Here is how the sausage is made. It sounds complicated, and it is, but none of the complicated parts are done by me.

<ApartmentPipeline/>

1 - **Floor plan.** The realtor's floor plan plus my photos. Claude reads the walls off the image and turns them into numbers.

2 - **Blender, via Python.** A script called `build.py` uses `bpy`, Blender's Python API, to extrude the walls, cut the doors and windows and place the furniture. Blender runs headless. Nobody ever looks at it. Poor Blender.

3 - **GLB export.** The scene is merged and exported as a single GLB file, the JPEG of 3D models.

4 - **three.js viewer.** A web page with three.js loads the GLB and adds everything that moves: walking, lights, doors, time of day. Then everything gets squashed into one single HTML file.

5 - **Commit and push to GitHub.** Why put it on the web at all? Because I need direct feedback from my girlfriend, and she's not going to install Blender. She has a life.

6 - **Netlify builds this very blog**, a Next.js site you know way too much about if you read the [migration post](/migration). The apartment lives at [/apartment](/apartment).

7 - **Girlfriend review.** She opens the link on her phone, she walks around, she looks at the sofa for a long time, and then she says:

## The showroom phase

Once the bedroom was solved, the rest of the apartment looked at me with big puppy eyes. The kitchen. The living room. The small room that will become my office, which in Danish is a *værelse*, which in Danish also means "room", because Danes ran out of words after inventing *hygge*.

So I asked Claude to scout the web for sofas, tables and chairs. Real ones, that you can actually buy in Denmark, with links, prices and every available size and colour.

It came back with spec sheets. It read the product pages, the PDFs, the tiny grey text under the photos. It knew the Besalu table from ILVA has a 105 cm round top that pulls apart for a 60 cm leaf. It knew the Kasami chair is 48 by 54.5 cm. It knew the price before the discount and after the discount. It knew more about Danish furniture than most Danes.

Every piece goes into the model with its real size, so you can see if it fits. Then you can swap it.

## The outside (a small accident)

Here's where things started to escalate.

Originally I only wanted a vague hint of what's outside the windows. Some volumes. Like the blue holograms in Star Wars: you get the idea of the Death Star, you don't need to count the windows.

But, by mistake, among the reference images I gave Claude, there was a photo of the outside of the building. And Claude took it as an order. It didn't render a hint of the outside. It rendered *the outside*. All of Grethe Ingemanns Vej. {/* Spelling: Grethe IngEmanns Vej. */}

When I realized what had happened, I had two options. Go back, or yes-and the robot.

I yes-anded the robot.

## Let there be light

The balcony faces east. Which means the morning sun comes straight into the living room, and I wanted to *see* that. Realistic light, realistic shadows, the full thing.

Claude delivered realistic light, all right. Danish light. Every single time I moved the time slider, the sky looked like a wet sock.

It took many, many iterations to explain that I didn't want an overcast sky. I wanted blue. Mediterranean blue. Sicilian August blue. The kind of sky Copenhagen sees maybe four days a year, and I wanted those four days on demand.

![Morning light from the east balcony](morning.jpg)

<FigureLabel>8:30 in the morning, Mediterranean edition. Copenhagen has filed a complaint.</FigureLabel>

Then, of course, I wanted a day-night cycle.

For the night sky, Claude first scattered some random white dots on a black background. And I said: don't be sloppy. I want the real Copenhagen night sky.

So now the stars are real. About a thousand of them, from an actual star catalogue, in the right positions for 55.7° North. If you stand on the balcony at midnight you can find the Big Dipper. In the model. Not in real life, in real life it's cloudy. {/* Fact check: ~1,000 stars brighter than magnitude 4.6 from the Yale Bright Star / Hipparcos data, via d3-celestial. Latitude of Copenhagen ≈ 55.7° N. */}

![The real night sky over Sluseholmen](nightsky.jpg)

<FigureLabel>These are the actual stars. I checked. Well, Claude checked. I nodded.</FigureLabel>

Now that I, as God, had made night follow day (the opposite of the Bible, read your Genesis), I needed lights.

I set up a realistic configuration of Philips Hue lights and light strips. The pendant over the dining table, the strip behind the TV, the spots under the kitchen cabinets, the fairy lights on the balcony. All of it, to measure the one thing that matters in Denmark: the hygge level.

![The TV at night](tv.jpg)

Each room has a light switch, and if you click it, a control panel opens. The first version was a classic developer interface: sliders, checkboxes, labels like `tvstrip_bri`. It looked like the cockpit of a Soviet submarine. I hated it.

So I asked for something more Apple-like. And the result is so nice that I'm thinking of turning it into a real app for my real house. {/* TK: true? The brief said "work in progress". */}

## The rabbit hole

I won't set foot in the apartment until January. I want the whole experience nailed before then, so I keep going back to the render. A new lamp here. A different chair there. What if the plant was 20 cm to the left.

The funny thing is, it's not even expensive. All of this barely dented my weekly Claude allowance. {/* TK: rough %, if you want a number. Numbers are funnier. */}

The bottleneck is never the AI. The bottleneck is always us. My girlfriend and me, deciding what's next, changing our minds, changing them back. And Claude just waits. It accepts our indecision. It takes the third reversal on the same sofa with the patience of a saint.

And every time we contradict ourselves, it tells us how absolutely right we are.

Everyone I show this to is impressed. Then, jokingly, they ask what's next. Are you going to render the rest of the neighbourhood? The rest of Copenhagen?

Joke's on them.

I'm not planning a horizontal expansion. I'm planning a vertical one.
